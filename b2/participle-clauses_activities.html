Participle Clauses

What is a participle clause?

A participle clause is a clause with no subject and no tense. It borrows both from the main clause, which is why it can only be used when the two clauses share a subject. All the examples below come from the reading a room article.

TypeExample
Present participle
-ing form · active		Walking in, do you instantly pick up on the feelings of the people there?
Past participle
past participle · passive		Called “reading a room” by psychologists, this is a skill that anyone can learn.
Perfect participle
having / having been		…someone is annoyed at having been called to the meeting at the last minute.
⚠ Why they matter: a participle clause is what you are left with when a full clause is reduced. If you want the reduction rules themselves, see Reduced Adverb Time Clauses, Reduced Adverb Cause-Effect Clauses and Reduced Relative Clauses. This page is about the three forms and the range of meanings they carry.

1 — Present participle (-ing): active meaning

The -ing clause always has an active meaning: the subject of the main clause does the action.

-ing form + … , + main clause

The same form does four different jobs. Only context tells you which.

JobExample
at the same timeWalking in, do you instantly pick up on the feelings of the people there?
one after the otherScanning the room, she noticed who was sitting next to whom.
a reasonNot knowing much about them, I still came up with a few ideas.
a result (clause comes second)The room was full of negative energy, making the meeting harder.
“Present” is only a label. An -ing clause has no tense of its own — it takes its time from the main clause. Walking in, do you pick up on the feelings? is present; Walking in, she saw three anxious faces is past. The form does not change.

Not every -ing is a participle clause. In the article, Knowing how to read the room is a vital workplace skill, the -ing phrase is the subject of the sentence — a gerund phrase, not an adverbial clause. Test it: you cannot put a comma after it.

2 — Past participle: passive meaning

The past participle clause always has a passive meaning: something is done to the subject of the main clause.

past participle + … , + main clause
Called “reading a room” by psychologists, this is a skill that anyone can learn.
Known as “microexpressions,” these can be the most accurate sign of how someone feels.
Asked directly, most people will explain how they are feeling.
The test: if you can put which is / which are or being in front of the participle, the passive form is the right one.
(which is) Called “reading a room” by psychologists… ✓   Calling “reading a room”… ✗

3 — Perfect participle: having + past participle

Use the perfect form when the action of the participle clause happened before the action of the main clause.

active: having + past participle  ·  passive: having been + past participle
Having done this a few times, you'll find it natural. — active
Having asked, you've placed the responsibility on them. — active
Someone is annoyed at having been called to the meeting at the last minute. — passive
Cause and sequence. The coursebook says perfect participles show causes and effects, and often they do: Having been called at the last minute, she was irritated. But they equally often mark nothing more than earlier time: Having done this a few times, you'll find it natural. Both readings are correct — what the form always signals is that one thing came first.

The same-subject rule

A participle clause has no subject of its own, so the reader automatically attaches it to the subject of the main clause. If that subject is wrong, the sentence becomes accidentally absurd — a dangling participle.

✗ Walking in, the room felt tense. — the room didn't walk in.
✓ Walking in, I felt the room was tense.
✓ When I walked in, the room felt tense. — or just don't reduce it.
⚠ Common mistakes:
✗ Walking in, the room felt tense. → ✓ Walking in, I felt the room was tense.
✗ Calling “reading a room” by psychologists… → ✓ Called “reading a room” by psychologists…
✗ Having did this a few times… → ✓ Having done this a few times…
✗ Annoyed at having called to the meeting… → ✓ annoyed at having been called to the meeting…
✗ Don't knowing much about them… → ✓ Not knowing much about them…
