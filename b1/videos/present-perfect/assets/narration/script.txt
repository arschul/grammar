The present perfect. The past that still matters... now.
Look at this timeline. "I lost my keys" is past simple. It's finished. It's just a fact about the past. But "I have lost my keys" connects the past to now. I still don't have my keys, so I can't get in. A past action, connected to now.
Here's the form. Subject, plus have or has, plus the past participle. Use have with I, you, we, and they. Use has with he, she, and it. When we speak, we usually contract: I've, she's. For negatives, say haven't or hasn't. For questions, put have or has first. Has he called?
Regular verbs add E D: worked, visited. But many common verbs are irregular. Go, gone. Be, been. See, seen. Eat, eaten. Do, done. Write, written. You need to memorize these.
Use number one: life experiences. Have you ever been to Japan? No, I've never been there. We don't say when it happened. It's about your life, up to now. Listen for ever, never, before, and how many times.
Use number two: something that started in the past, and is still true. She has lived in Lima since twenty twenty. She still lives there. Use for with a period of time, like for six years. Use since with a starting point, like since twenty twenty.
Use number three: a recent action with a result now. I've just broken my phone. So now, I have no phone. Have you finished yet? Yes, I've already finished! Look for just, already, yet, and recently.
So when do we use the past simple instead? With a finished time, like yesterday, last week, or two days ago, use past simple. I saw it last Friday. With no finished time, use present perfect. I've seen that movie.
Here's a common mistake. Don't say, "I have seen him yesterday." Yesterday is a finished time. So say, "I saw him yesterday."
Past, plus now, equals present perfect. Now you've learned it!
